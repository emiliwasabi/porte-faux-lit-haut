Webbed Gardens
Gathering as practice: Digital gardens and the politics of collection in an age of algorithmic flux
(image: gardening2.png)

I. The Garden
In the past, people enjoyed looking at the sky, but that was only because they had nothing better to do. They didn't have websites. It's hard to say which is true. Either the sky is more valuable than a website, or a website is more valuable than the sky. If I were to add up the time I spent browsing websites and the time I spent looking at the sky, then my life would clearly answer the question of what was most valuable to me. The blog has always made me feel at home. 

You look after a website just as you look after a garden. But it's software. So it's not outside. So it's not inside either. It's nowhere, really. It's just there. Very few things run continuously. Most things switch on and off. Shops open and close. Cars run until they run out of petrol. But without warning, there were links everywhere.
This research takes its starting point from a simple, universal act: that of gathering. We are all collectors. Before the server, before the algorithm, there was the act of accumulation. Drawing on [Ursula K. Le Guin's](carrierbag7.jpg|Ursula K. Le Guin, The Carrier Bag of Fiction) provocation that the first human tool was not the spear but the basket; a receptacle before a weapon, this thesis proposes that collecting is not merely an archival impulse, but a form of resistance, and a design methodology.
The web once carried a fundamentally cooperative ideology: [H.G. Wells' 1937 vision of a 'World Brain'](worldbrain1.png|H.G. Wells, World Brain), [Ted Nelson's hypertext](hypertext1.gif|Ted Nelson, Hypertext), the horizontal logic of interconnected knowledge. What platform capitalism replaced it with is a closed garden optimized for retention, not discovery. A feed instead of a link, an algorithm instead of navigation. In this context, the digital garden emerges as a counter-practice: a handmade, personal space on the web where ideas are planted, linked, and allowed to grow, outside the pressures of performance and engagement metrics.
This thesis investigates how the design of collection spaces can emancipate itself from the 'flux' to propose an information topology that favors sedimented memory over passive consumption. Through a theoretical framework spanning media history, design practice, personal stories and grounded in the construction of an index of indexes (a personal research tool cataloguing existing digital gardens) it asks what it means to become an architect of one's own digital space, and how gathering is an attitude. The answer, ultimately, is not a new platform. It is a posture: tending, rather than scrolling.
II. On Archiving & Gathering
"Archiving" presupposes an archivist, a hand that collects and classifies. An archive has meaning within its context; it belongs to a social environment. "Gathering" involves collecting objects and sharing them. Objects survive only because we want them to; we accumulate our possessions for personal reasons. Archives are often interpreted as a means of presenting the past in the present. At the same time, they are machines that carry the present into the future. Our species passes on acquired and inherited information from one generation to the next. 
In the field of design practice, collecting is an essential step. Designers collect content for publications and typefaces. Gathering visual inspiration is crucial for honing our skills and aesthetic sensibility. These ideas serve as vessels suited to various forms of content. Design projects cover a wide range of subjects, extending beyond the expertise of the designers themselves. Curation enables us to gather relevant content from various experts, thanks to the searchable nature of the web and the ease of accessing related documents via hyperlinks.
We bring things together because we forget. We bring things together because knowledge is not self-evident: it requires effort, selection, and a choice as to what should be placed alongside what. Today, the stakes involved in this choice have never been higher. The problem is no longer scarcity. An overwhelming and incessant flood of content that evolves too quickly to be absorbed, is too fragile to be reliable, and too compartmentalised to be interconnected. In this context, collecting is not a pastime; it is a form of manifesto, and I have always loved rebellion. 
In 1937, H.G. Wells envisaged a 'World Brain', a vast, interconnected encyclopaedia that would make humanity's accumulated knowledge accessible to anyone seeking it, in real time. Wells had realised that the crisis of his time stemmed not from a lack of knowledge, but from a failure to organise and disseminate it. The links were missing. Twenty-eight years later, Ted Nelson coined the term 'hypertext', and Wells's dream was given a mechanism: the ability to follow a thread from one idea to another, across documents. Wells's World Brain and Nelson's hypertext share the same fundamental insight: knowledge is a network. Its value lies not in accumulation, but in connection. The early Web embodied a fundamentally cooperative ideology: the conviction that the network, left to its own horizontal logic, would enable knowledge to be disseminated more equitably than any institution had previously managed to do. Then came the platforms.
Not all at once, and not without offering something in return. But gradually, the Web (that jungle of personal pages, web rings and hand-coded directories) has been replaced by walled gardens designed not for exploration, but for retention. The news feed has replaced the link. The algorithm has replaced navigation. Passive consumption has replaced active searching. The feed has replaced the garden. They have stolen our links. 
And with them, something essential has been lost: the possibility of losing oneself, of stumbling upon something one wasn't looking for. What has been lost in this transition is precisely serendipity, the productive accident, the detour that turns out to be the destination. Platforms optimised for engagement cannot afford serendipity. Surprise is inefficient. Discovery cannot be monetised. In response to this, a counter-trend has emerged. 
A digital garden is an old impulse taking on a new form. The digital garden is a practical, makeshift, fragile and stubbornly personal response to the algorithmic capture of the web. We must draw some meaning from the way knowledge circulates, survives and is passed on between people who share it. At its core, this is a sociological question: about infrastructure, about practice, about what it means to bring things together, and about what it means to collect.
